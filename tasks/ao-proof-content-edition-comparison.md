# Proof content: the guard height article

**Prefix:** `ao-` — high priority ops, actionable now. The corpus and every
surface this article needs are on production. The subject is chosen and
checked against the database (see **The facts**).

## Goal

Write one public article that answers a question a code consultant asks every
month, and that no free source answers. The article is the artefact a reader
forwards to a colleague. It does two jobs at the same time:

1. It proves the product without a trial and without a demo call.
2. It ranks for the long-tail question the product answers.

Do not write a second article until this one has run for 60 days.

## The hook

Do not open with four texts side by side. A side-by-side is the payoff, not
the reason a reader starts.

Open with a question, and do not tell the reader what they think:

> What is the minimum guard height for a stair inside a house in Ontario?

**Answer it five times, and split the stair.** An earlier draft gave one
number per date and said most people answer 900 mm. Both were wrong. The
assumption is unearned, and one number per date is not what the code says: the
1997 sentence sets 800 mm on the flight **and 900 mm above landings** in the
same breath, and from 2014 to 2022 the landing stood 170 mm above the flight.
So the opening is a table with a row per distinct answer and a column each for
the flight and the landing. The reader draws the conclusion; the page states
facts.

| In force | Flight | Landing | Spiral stair | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 Apr 1998 to 30 Dec 2006 | 800 mm | 900 mm | 800 mm | The only text that allows 800 mm |
| 31 Dec 2006 to 31 Dec 2013 | 900 mm | 900 mm | 900 mm | The flight rises 100 mm; the article changes number |
| 1 Jan 2014 to 31 Dec 2021 | 900 mm | **1 070 mm** | 900 mm | A new sentence raises the landing |
| 1 Jan 2022 to 31 Dec 2024 | 900 mm | 900 mm | **1 070 mm** | The landing drops 170 mm; spiral stairs go the other way |
| 1 Jan 2025 to today | 900 mm | 900 mm | 900 mm | OBC 2024; the spiral-stair carve-out does not come with it |

**Derive every spiral cell from a sentence. Never copy the column beside it.**
The figures happen to match the flight column until 2022, and that is a result,
not a method. Each one comes from a different place:

| In force | Spiral | The sentence that gets you there |
|---|---|---|
| 1998–2006 | 800 mm | 9.8.8.2.(4) — "guards for **stairs** within dwelling units". The 1997 article never uses the word "flight" |
| 2006–2014 | 900 mm | 9.8.8.3.(2) — "all guards within dwelling units", shape-blind |
| 2014–2022 | 900 mm | (2) again |
| 2022–2024 | 1 070 mm | (2) excludes it, (4) cannot reach it, so (1) |
| 2025– | 900 mm | 2024 (2) — no spiral exclusion, and shape-blind |

**Sentence (2) is shape-blind in every edition**, which is what makes four of
the five cells robust. It is also why the flights-versus-spiral question only
ever matters in one window: before 2022 sentence (2) catches every guard inside
a dwelling unit whatever the shape, so it makes no difference whether (4)
reaches a spiral stair. From 2022 it does matter — and that is exactly the day
the code answers it in 9.8.3.1.

Two wrong drafts to learn from. The first copied the flight column into the
spiral cells. The second, correcting it, went the other way and proposed "no
Part 9 rule" for the pre-2022 cells, on the ground that a helix does not fit
9.8.3.1.(2)'s closed list. **That confused two questions**: whether a spiral
stair is a permitted *configuration* (9.8.5. / 9.8.3.1., arguable) and how high
its *guard* must be (9.8.8.x, answerable). The column asks the second. A
builder still has to set a number, and the code gives one.

**The date range is the row, not a sample permit year.** An earlier table
picked a year per row ("1999", "2008") and put the edition in its own column.
Both were re-drawing the corpus rather than reading it. A sample year says
nothing the range does not, and the edition name is what the section under the
row is about. The fourth column is what the amendment did, and it links to the
section that proves it.

**The table gives one figure per cell.** The 2014 landing cell went through
"900 or 1 070 mm" and then "900 mm, 1 070 on one reading" before reaching
1 070 mm. Both hedges came from the same error, below. A table that hedges is
a table that answers nothing, and the table is where a hurried reader stops.

**920 mm is disposed of in the prose, not in the table.** Two of these texts
set 920 mm for a required exit stair. An exit runs from a floor area to
outdoors, and a stair inside a house is not one, so the figure is not an answer
to this question — but a reader meets it in the redlines and needs to be told
why it is absent from the table. The exception is a house with a secondary
suite, where a shared stair can be part of a required exit. **This reading is
on the list for the building official.**

**Two minimums do not contradict each other. The higher one is the
requirement.** This is the rule the whole 2014 answer turns on, and two drafts
of this card got it wrong before Rob pointed it out (14 August 2026). Both
drafts treated sentence (2) — "not less than 900 mm" inside a dwelling unit —
and sentence (5)(b) — "not less than 1 070 mm around landings" — as a conflict
needing a tiebreak, and went hunting for one. There is no conflict. A guard
1 070 mm high satisfies both. So at a landing inside a house, from 1 January
2014 to 31 December 2021, the requirement is **1 070 mm**, and the flight
beside it stays at 900 mm.

**What creates a relaxation is the except-formula, and only that.** Sentence
(1) opens "Except as provided in Sentences (2) to (6)", which is what lets
sentence (2)'s 900 mm displace the general 1 070 mm — without it, sentence (2)
would have nothing to do, since 1 070 already satisfies it. Nothing of the
kind sits between sentence (2) and sentence (5); sentence (5) is excepted from
sentence (6) alone. The 1997 article shows the same drafter writing
displacement out where it is meant: its sentence (3) opens "Except as provided
in Sentence (4)", and (4) is what drops a stair inside a dwelling unit to
800 mm.

**The Part 3 history is off the page, and should stay off.** The 1 070 mm was
Part 3's first, as one sentence about exit stairs (OBC 2012 B 3.4.6.6.(2), and
before it OBC 2006 B 3.4.6.5.(2)); Part 9 split it into (a) and (b) and put
"for required exit stairs" in (a) only. That evidence was gathered to argue
clause (b) meant something narrower than it said — the second wrong draft,
which read a limit into (b) to escape a conflict that was never there. When
that argument died the section was reframed as "history" and kept, then cut
for real, and the `ASIDES` machinery in the view went with it.

Evidence gathered for a discarded argument does not transfer. It was selected
to answer a question nobody is asking any more, and keeping it meant writing a
new reason for it to exist. **Do not bring it back.**

**1 January 2022 is a relaxation of 170 mm, not a tidy-up.** Part 9's sentence
(5) was revoked — it still carries its number and reads *Reserved*, and
sentence (1) now excepts (2), (3), (4) and (6) only — so a landing that needed
1 070 mm on 31 December 2021 needed 900 mm the next day.

**The same amendment sends spiral stairs the other way, and the corpus proves
the package.** O. Reg. 88/19 changes three provisions on that one day:
9.8.3.1. is rewritten so a stair consists of "straight flights, curved
flights, **or spiral stairs**"; 9.8.4.5A. is created, requiring the outer
**handrail** at not less than 1 070 mm; and 9.8.8.3.(2) drops guards serving
spiral stairs. Sentence (4) then cannot reach a spiral stair — it has no
flights — and sentence (2) no longer covers it, so sentence (1) governs at
1 070 mm, the same figure the new handrail article sets.

Two consequences worth knowing and not currently on the page. The same
amendment narrowed sentence (7) from "guards for **stairs and landings**" to
"guards for **flights**", which leaves a spiral stair guard with a required
height and **no stated way to measure it** — and the old method could not work
anyway, since a helical nosing line has no straight tangent. And sentence (3)
has no spiral carve-out, so an *exterior* spiral stair serving a house within
1 800 mm of grade still gets 900 mm.

**The subtitle counts nothing.** It said "Six answers since 1998", which
counted texts and called them answers. It now states the fact: the code sets
the flight and the landing separately, and for eight years they were 170 mm
apart.

Then show the second, larger error. The provision **number** moved:

- In OBC 1997, guard height is **9.8.8.2.**
- From 31 December 2006, guard height is **9.8.8.3.**, and **9.8.8.2.** means
  *Loads on Guards*.

So a report that cites 9.8.8.2. for guard height is right for a 1999 permit
and wrong for a 2008 permit. The citation still resolves. The citation now
points at a different rule. This is the failure the product prevents, and a
reader can check it in one click.

## The facts

All of these come from the production database, on 12 August 2026. Re-read
them before you publish; do not copy a number from this card into the
article.

**Seven texts, one question, 1998 to today.** Guard height has six versions
in the corpus, and a seventh text governs new work now:

| Edition | Provision | Version | In force from | In the corpus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OBC 1997 | 9.8.8.2. | 0 | 6 April 1998 | Yes |
| OBC 2006 | 9.8.8.3. | 0 | 31 December 2006 | Yes |
| OBC 2006 | 9.8.8.3. | 1 | 1 January 2010 | Yes |
| OBC 2012 | 9.8.8.3. | 0 | 1 January 2014 | Yes |
| OBC 2012 | 9.8.8.3. | 1 | 1 July 2017 | Yes |
| OBC 2012 | 9.8.8.3. | 2 | 1 January 2022 | Yes |
| OBC 2024 | 9.8.8.3. | — | 1 January 2025 | No — see below |

The stored windows are half-open. Use `core.seo.last_governed_day` for every
"until" date in the article. Do not subtract a day by hand.

Four changes carry the story:

- **The 800 mm guard is gone.** OBC 1997 sentence (4) permits 800 mm for a
  stair inside a dwelling unit. OBC 2006 sentence (2) requires 900 mm for
  every guard inside a dwelling unit. A guard built at 800 mm in 2003 met the
  code of its day.
- **Sentence (5) lived for eight years.** OBC 2012 introduces it on 1 January
  2014: (a) 920 mm for a required exit stair, (b) 1 070 mm around landings.
  Clause (b) applies alongside sentence (2), and the higher figure governs, so
  every Part 9 landing is 1 070 mm for that whole window. The version of
  1 January 2022 replaces the sentence with "Reserved" and the landing drops
  to 900 mm.
- **The 1 500 mm rule is new in 2012.** An exterior stair more than 10 m
  above the ground needs a 1 500 mm guard. Neither earlier edition says this.
- **The measurement rule changed twice.** OBC 1997 measures from a line
  through the outside edges of the nosings. The amendment of 1 January 2010
  adds "from the top of the guard to". OBC 2012 changes the line to a
  tangent, and the version of 2022 rewrites the sentence again.

**"The 2012 code says" is not a sentence.** OBC 2012 holds three different
guard-height texts. That single fact is the strongest argument on the page,
because it defeats the reader's own workaround of keeping one PDF.

**A page scan exists** for OBC 1997 9.8.8.2. (one page image). Show it. A
scan of the paper edition is evidence in a way reset text is not.

## The current answer: OBC 2024

The article must give today's answer. A reader who cannot find the current
rule stops trusting the historical ones. OBC 2024 is not in the corpus, so
the writer takes these numbers from the sources named here.

**The 2024 code is one page.** O. Reg. 163/24 adopts the National Building
Code of Canada 2020 and an amendment document. Quote section 1 exactly:

> ... known as CCBFC NRCC-CONST-56435E, National Building Code of Canada
> 2020, (First Printing), as amended by the document entitled "Ontario
> Amendments to the National Building Code of Canada 2020", dated July 17,
> 2026 and issued by the Ministry of Municipal Affairs and Housing, are
> together adopted as the building code.

Guard height keeps the number **9.8.8.3.** Ontario changes two sentences and
leaves the rest to the national code.

**Ontario replaces sentence (1).** Quote from the amendment document:

> (1) Except as provided in Sentences (2) to (3.1), all guards shall be not
> less than 1 070 mm high.

**Ontario adds sentence (3.1).** Quote exactly, including the spacing:

> (3.1) The height of guards for exterior stairs and landings more than 10 m
> above adjacent ground level shall be not less than 1500 mm.

**The consolidated article reads as follows.** Taken from the 2024 Building
Code Compendium, Volume 1, Division B Part 9, at
`CodeChronicleMapping/data/inputs/pdfs/ontario.ca/301880.pdf`. Sentences (2),
(3) and (4) are the national text; (1) and (3.1) are Ontario's.

> **9.8.8.3. Height of Guards**
> (1) Except as provided in Sentences (2) to (3.1), all guards shall be not
> less than 1 070 mm high.
> (2) All guards within dwelling units or within houses with a secondary
> suite including their common spaces shall be not less than 900 mm high.
> (3) Exterior guards serving not more than one dwelling unit or a house with
> a secondary suite including their common spaces shall be not less than
> 900 mm high where the walking surface served by the guard is not more than
> 1 800 mm above the finished ground level.
> (3.1) The height of guards for exterior stairs and landings more than 10 m
> above adjacent ground level shall be not less than 1 500 mm.
> (4) The height of guards for flights of steps shall be measured vertically
> from the top of the guard to a line drawn through the tread nosing served by
> the guard.

**The 900 mm allowance for a flight of steps is gone.** This is the change
that matters, and the article's second strongest fact. OBC 2012 sentence (4)
read "Guards for flights, except in required exit stairs, shall be not less
than 900 mm high". OBC 2024 has no such sentence. A guard on a flight that
is not inside a dwelling unit, and does not serve one dwelling unit outside,
now goes to 1 070 mm.

**Check this claim harder than any other in the article.** It says work that
passed in 2024 fails in 2025. Put it to a building official before you
publish.

**The 920 mm exit-stair guard did not come back.** That number is Ontario's
own. It lived from 1 January 2014 to 1 January 2022 and appears nowhere in
the 2024 code.

**Two other quiet changes.** The spiral-stair exception in the 2022 text is
gone. Secondary suites and their common spaces now sit beside dwelling units
in (2) and (3).

**The local copy is not current.** `301880.pdf` consolidates to 16 January
2025 (O. Reg. 5/25). The regulation now names an amendment document dated
17 July 2026. The Ontario amendments to 9.8.8.3. are word-for-word the same
in the amendment documents of 8 October 2024 and 1 April 2026, so the
wording is stable across that span. Confirm the gap from April 2026 before
you publish.

**Three amendment documents were checked**, dated 8 October 2024, 1 April
2026 and 17 July 2026. All three amend 9.8.8.3. in the same words. The last
is the document O. Reg. 163/24 currently adopts, so the article cites that
one.

The section number is **not** stable across documents, and the article cites
it: the guard-height amendment is section 341 in the October 2024 document
and section 344 in both 2026 documents. Re-read the section number whenever
the adopted document changes, not only the wording.

**Where to get the current document.** The ministry does not put it on
e-Laws. Three routes, in the order to try them:

1. `https://www.codenews.ca/OBC/OBC2024/Ontario%20Amendments%20to%20the%20National%20Building%20Code%20of%20Canada%20-%20YYYY-MM-DD.pdf`,
   where the date is the document's own date. CodeNews mirrors each release
   at that address, and the date is the one named in O. Reg. 163/24 s. 1.
2. `Ontario.Amendment.Document@ontario.ca` sends the current document by an
   auto-reply. This is the ministry's own route and the one to cite.
3. The CodeNews newsletter announces each release, which is how you learn a
   new date exists.

**Read the date out of the regulation first**, not out of a file listing.
Section 1 of O. Reg. 163/24 names the adopted document by date, so the
regulation is what says which file is law. The Word copy at
`https://www.ontario.ca/laws/docs/240163_e.doc` carries that text; the e-Laws
page itself loads it with a script and a fetch returns an empty shell.

## Why the current answer is hard to get, and why that helps

This is the strongest ending available, and it is true.

- Ontario's regulation is free, and it contains no guard height.
- The Ontario Amendment Document is free on request, and it gives sentence
  (1) and sentence (3.1) only.
- Sentences (2), (3) and (4) come from NBC 2020, which the National Research
  Council owns. The Building Code Compendium joins the two, and Publications
  Ontario supplies it as a free download.
- The full text no longer appears on e-Laws at all.

So a reader cannot assemble today's rule from the regulation alone, and no
free source holds the 2003 rule. State the first fact carefully and without
complaint. It is the reason the product exists.

## What the Compendium licence permits

Read this before you decide what the article quotes. The terms are in
`CodeChronicleMapping/data/inputs/pdfs/ontario.ca/301881.pdf`, page 1, dated
May 2024. **This card reports the terms. It does not give legal advice, and
somebody must take this to counsel before the article ships.**

The licence turns on one test:

> A non-commercial use is where you provide free access to the 2024 Building
> Code Compendium's material. If free access to the 2024 Building Code
> Compendium's material is not provided, your reproduction ... is considered
> by the Ministry of Municipal Affairs and Housing to be a commercial use and
> is only permitted under a licence.

Three points follow, and they are not the same point:

- **A free article is the permitted case.** The article is public and costs
  nothing to read. On the face of the terms, that is a non-commercial use.
- **A gated edition is the licensed case.** Putting OBC 2024 behind the Pro
  subscription is a commercial use, and it needs a licence from
  `buildingtransformation@ontario.ca`. This is the request to make, and it is
  a separate job from the article.
- **The national text is a separate owner.** The licence grants "use of Crown
  copyrighted material". NBC 2020 belongs to the National Research Council.
  So sentences (2), (3) and (4) may sit outside this grant even though they
  print inside the Compendium.

Conditions the article must meet if it quotes:

- Reproduce accurately and without modification.
- Do not present the product as an official version of the Ontario
  government.
- Use no Government of Ontario trademark, logo or symbol.
- Carry the statement: `© King's Printer for Ontario, 2024. Reproduced with
  permission.`

**The safe article, if counsel is slow:** quote sentence (1) and sentence
(3.1) from the Ontario Amendment Document, which the ministry publishes for
anybody, and state the remaining rules as facts with a citation rather than
as quoted text. A number is a fact. The sentence that carries it is
somebody's writing.

## The transition: OBC 2012 governs buildings on site now

Write this carefully. OBC 2012 is closed to new work, and it still governs
work that is under way.

O. Reg. 163/24 section 2 keeps OBC 2012, **as it read on 31 December 2024**,
in force for construction where:

- a permit was issued on or before 31 December 2024; or
- the drawings were substantially complete on or before 31 December 2024 and
  somebody applied for a permit on or before 31 March 2025.

Construction must start within six months after the permit is issued.

**Both doors closed in 2025.** No new project can come under OBC 2012 now.
Do not write that OBC 2012 is "still in force" without that limit, because a
reader checks the dates and stops trusting the article.

What continues is the project that already entered. The permit governs the
building for the length of the build. So a framing inspection this month, an
occupancy permit, a permit revision and any dispute about that work all read
OBC 2012 as it read on 31 December 2024. Large buildings run for years.

"As it read on 31 December 2024" is the version of 1 January 2022. A reader
with such a permit needs a text that e-Laws no longer shows as current, and
the product holds it. Put this near the call to action.

There is a second, larger reason a reader needs old text, and the article
should name it: an alteration, an insurance claim or a lawsuit asks what the
code required on the day the building went up. That question has no closing
date.

## Structure

**The page is built out of changes, not out of texts.** This replaced a shape
that gave the current answer, then the analysis, then all six texts in a row at
the foot. Three things were wrong with it. The reader did the diffing, which is
the product's job and the thing worth showing. The prose asserted what each
amendment did and the evidence sat six screens away. And a text dump ends the
page on its least readable part.

So: one text in full, then one section per amendment, each showing the redline
of exactly that change, each stating what it did to the answer — including the
two amendments that did nothing to it. Every table row links to its section.

1. **The question**, asked in one sentence. Then flight and landing defined,
   the five-row table, and the 920 mm disposal.
2. **1998.** The oldest text in full, with the attestation band. The 1997 scan
   is here.
3. **31 December 2006.** The flight rises to 900 mm, and the article changes
   number. Both facts in one redline.
4. **1 January 2010.** A measurement change that moves no number. It is on the
   page *because* it moves nothing: that is the fact a reader with two PDFs
   cannot get without reading both in full.
5. **1 January 2014.** Sentence (5) arrives and the landing goes to 1 070 mm.
   State the composition rule outright, and show that sentence (5) is excepted
   from sentence (6) alone. **The page does not currently say this.** A reader
   sees (2) at 900 mm in the redline and (5)(b) at 1 070 mm, and gets no rule
   for combining them — which is what four drafts of this card got wrong.
6. **1 July 2017.** An exterior rewording. Also moves nothing here.
7. **1 January 2022.** Sentence (5) is revoked and reads *Reserved*; spiral
   stairs leave sentence (2) and go to 1 070 mm.
8. **1 January 2025.** The 2024 article, quoted, because it is not in the
   corpus. Say so plainly, once.
9. **How to check this yourself.** Link to the Sources page and the
   verification-rail guide. Invite the reader to disagree.
10. **One call to action.** Search your own date. Not "sign up".

**Two sections came out on 14 August 2026 and are not coming back by
accident.** "Why the 2012 text is still live work" carried the permit-window
rules — OBC 2012 governs buildings under construction now — and with it gone
the page never says why an old text matters to a live file. That is the one
removal worth a second look. The Part 3 history went for the reasons above.

**Today's answer no longer comes first.** The old structure put it second so a
reader wanting only the current number could leave satisfied. The table now
does that job in one row, at the top, and it does it for every date rather than
one — which is the article's actual claim.

## How it is built

**The corpus text renders live; the analysis is hand-written.** The article
asks the database for the six versions and renders them through the partials
the reading pages use. A quoted copy is a transcription, and a transcription
never learns that the corpus changed. Live rendering also puts the real
attestation band beside the text, which is the thing no competitor can show.

**The changes render through the comparison page's own components.** Each
amendment section includes `provenance/_compare_pane.html` with the diff
`api.formatters._diff_html_content` produces, above the side rows that identify
both versions. Those rows used to live inside `partials/_compare_body.html`;
this work moved them to `partials/_compare_sides.html` so `/compare/` and the
article share one answer to "what must be stated about a version before a
redline of it is evidence". The article leaves out the comparison page's
timeline, pairing basis and legend, and supplies those in prose instead; the
link at the foot of each section opens the page that carries them.

**The redline floor is applied here too** (`core.compare.REDLINE_FLOOR`). Every
pair currently clears it — the closest is 1997 against 2006 at 38% — but an
article that redlined a pair `/compare/` refuses to redline would be showing
the reader something the product does not do. The corpus check fails if a pair
drops under the floor.

**A comparison link is gated on both sides.** `/compare/` refuses a pair with
one side outside the free tier, so most of these links open the teaser for an
anonymous reader. The article marks each one rather than letting the reader
find out by clicking. The *texts* still render in full, because the view sets
no `locked_edition_name`.

**Two checks pin the facts the prose depends on**, because one cannot do it
alone. Live rendering has a single failure mode: the hand-written analysis
says "800 mm" while the rendered block no longer does.

- `core/tests/test_guard_height_article.py` builds its own corpus and proves
  the **view** surfaces each claim. It cannot prove the shipped corpus still
  says these things, because a fixture that asserts its own data is circular.
- `python manage.py verify_guard_height_article` reads a **real** database
  and fails when a claim stops holding. Run it after `load_edition`, and
  before the article ships or goes out by email.

**The page quotes the 2024 article in full, all five sentences.** It went
through two earlier shapes, and both were worse. First it quoted only Ontario's
(1) and (3.1); a reader met "Except as provided in Sentences (2) to (3.1)" and
then met (3.1), with the gap invisible and unexplained, exactly where the
article's sharpest claim lives. Then it showed five rows with the national
three stated in our words. That closed the gap and kept a different problem: a
paraphrase of a rule is an argument about the rule, and this page's whole claim
is what the article does and does not contain. **Rob decided to quote it**
(13 August 2026). Two things followed from that decision:

- **Quote from the Compendium, and use its spacing.** It prints
  "1 500 mm" where the earlier transcription of the Amendment Document read
  "1500 mm". This closes the open question about that figure.
- **Carry the attribution.** `© King's Printer for Ontario, 2024. Reproduced
  with permission.` sits in the quotation's footer. The other licence
  conditions are properties of the page rather than strings: quote accurately,
  claim no official status (the closing note does this), use no Ontario
  trademark. `test_it_carries_the_compendium_attribution` pins the string;
  nothing can pin the rest.

**Do not test the allowance with a substring.** Every edition carries "not
more than 1 800 mm above the finished ground level", so `"800 mm" in html`
finds the 1997 allowance in all three editions and reports that it never went
away. `allows_800_mm` anchors on "less than" instead. The test fixture keeps
the 1 800 mm sentence on purpose, so a check that falls into the trap fails
in the test rather than in public.

**The article does not need a gate exception, and must not add one.**
`core.access` answers one question: may this user open this *edition*. The
partials do not ask it. A view decides and sets `locked_edition_name`, and
the partials render what the view says. So the article view leaves that flag
unset and the 1997 text renders.

Do not add a provision-level or version-level axis to `core/access.py`. That
module works because there is one question to ask. A second axis means every
other surface either asks a second question or silently does not, which is
drift in the module written to stop drift. This article would be its only
caller.

**The permalinks stay locked, and the article says so.** A 1997 link opens
the teaser. That is correct, and it is where a reader decides to subscribe.

**The page sets `meta_title` and `meta_description` in the view.** Do not
override `{% block title %}` and stop there. `base.html` resolves the title
as `{% firstof meta_title default_title %}`, and
`templates/partials/_social_meta.html` builds the Open Graph and Twitter tags
from the same two variables. A block changes the browser tab only, so every
forwarded link would carry the generic site card. This article travels by
forwarding, so the card is its first impression.

Set `canonical_path` as well, and check the card on a **sent** message. The
compose box draws a text-only preview that looks broken.

**The page carries no form.** One `{% csrf_token %}` attaches a `Set-Cookie`
header and no shared cache stores the response.

**The call to action searches a free date.** It ran at 1 June 1999, which is
OBC 1997 and outside `FREE_TIER_CODE_NAMES`, so the article's one button sent
a first-time reader straight to the locked-edition page. It now runs at
1 June 2008, inside OBC 2006. `test_the_call_to_action_searches_a_free_date`
reads the date out of the rendered link and checks it against the editions the
tier actually names, so moving either one without the other fails. This is the
opposite decision from the specimens above, and deliberately: the article
*shows* gated text on the page, because that is the proof; it must not *send*
a reader to a gated page, because that is the invitation.

`verify_guard_height_article` passes against a local corpus carrying all three
editions (6 versions). The page renders with the band and the 1997 scan on the
opening text, five redlines in the chain, and the 2022 redline showing sentence
(5) going to "Reserved".

**The tests pin behaviour, never content.** Four tests asserted phrases from
the article. Between them they broke six times on rewordings that left every
claim intact, and found no defect once. A test earns its place by catching an
accident; prose is changed on purpose. They are gone, and the rule is written
at the top of the test file.

Two exceptions stay strict, because they are obligations rather than
judgements: the King's Printer attribution string, and the 2024 quotation
being reproduced in full. Accurate reproduction is a condition of the
Compendium licence.

A claim about what the *code* says belongs in
`verify_guard_height_article`, which reads the corpus. A claim about what the
*page* says belongs to the author.
